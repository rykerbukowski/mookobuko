What are the Balkans exactly? I pictured rugged mountains and cliffs that drop off into the ocean. War. Civil unrest. Communism. That's what most people, people who lived through the 90s, associate with the term. The region takes its name from the Balkan Mountains, stretched throughout Bulgaria. Generally they include the countries pictured here. Ryker and I decided to make the jump to the Balkans for banal reasons: money and visas.

<img loading="lazy" src="/media/posts/22/gallery/balkans-route-map.jpg" alt="Our red route line looping through the Balkans, from Croatia down through Albania and Greece and back up to Romania" />

On the money front: July and August are some of the worst times to travel, because everyone else is traveling. All of Europe is on holiday and does a massive house-swap, but on a country-wide scale. The British are in Bulgaria, the Irish head to Italy, the Italians to Ireland, the Germans are everywhere. And for some reason you can always count on the Australians (I can't figure out when their "working season" would be) to be traversing the globe. Generally speaking, Europeans move to coastal destinations, usually in cheaper European countries than their own (and I am calling it now that the Nordics are going to be the new Greece for summertime holidays if global warming keeps rearing its ugly head). Anyways, Ryker and I are caught up in the whirlwind of summer travel that is overtaking the continent. The Balkans and most of Eastern Europe are more thrifty than the classic West.

<img loading="lazy" src="/media/posts/22/gallery/schengen-zone-map.jpg" alt="Map of Europe color-coded by EU and Schengen membership" />

The other and much more dominant factor was the infamous Schengen Zone. If you don't know what the Schengen Zone is, it's a pact amongst the entire European Union plus Switzerland and Iceland to exclude Americans, Australians and now the British from the party. Incredibly rude if you ask me (an American). A couple of kids, however, have yet to ever get invited to this party. Not for lack of trying; all of these kids apply and beg year after year for EU membership, but it has yet to be granted. Those places are a goldmine for travelers looking to duck out of the EU and not accidentally get deported or arrested for overstaying a visa. Which is how we ended up in Albania.

## Albania

We flew into Tirana, Albania's capital city, after a short and successful experiment with pre-booking a taxi using booking.com. You can imagine that after traveling for 8 months across almost 20 countries, we have racked up quite the tab on Airbnb. From hotels, to apartments, to hostels, we can never seem to book the right place. That's travel; something is always off, or wrong, or hard to deal with, but we have been dealing with it. And in dealing with it, I have been developing a taste, a list, of what my ideal accommodation would be. The frustrating part of booking things online is that everyone seems to lie: a listed AC unit that turns out to be a fan, an advertised king/queen/full bed that's really just two twins with a massive sheet thrown on top, "high speed wifi" clocking in at 0.2 Mbps, a "sea view" that looks across a concrete parking lot. I have become a detective looking through booking information, zooming into listing photos to make sure I can really see an AC unit, double and triple checking what I can. But at the end of the day, with the speed we are traveling, mistakes happen, listings lie, and I am just trying my best.

So through trial and error and error and error, I now have grand opinions about things that I took for granted in my former sedentary life. I now deeply care about showers having doors or curtains, and about the length and texture of the curtain. I care about sheets and blankets and thread count. I care that the shower and toilet are separate from one another, I care that the bed is a mattress and not a box spring, I care to sleep with a blanket and not just a sheet, I care that the little cups they give you for instant coffee can fit more than 4oz of liquid, I care about having a bedside table and outlets in reasonable places, and I care about having a mirror, preferably full-length, so I can stare at the very bourgeois person I am becoming.

It's happened: traveling has made me aware of my American Privilege™, and greatly increased my gratitude for all I have been given in my life. A thank you to my parents for all you have afforded me; I took it for granted and I never will again.

With that said, let me describe to you my perfect accommodation (at least while long-term traveling).

- The bed fits both my husband and me comfortably: 4 pillows, a fluffy comforter, no bedbugs, clean white sheets without stains. (You would be disturbed to know the amount of blood, hair, unidentifiable splotches and smears that plague the rooms we walk into.)
- A kitchen with a fridge, hotplate, at least one pan, knife, and cutting board. (Bonus points if the fridge also works. Can you tell my standards are on the ground?)
- A balcony. This doubles as a great place to have coffee in the morning and is a godsend for drying clothes. Bonus points if the view is nice or good for people-watching.
- A bathroom with a functional toilet and a separate enclosed shower with enough space that you can reach up and wash your hair without slamming your hands into the wall. Ideally the shower has a shelf for soap and such and a little ledge for shaving. (I have actually given up on having a ledge to shave (think like the bathtub edge in our very American shower/tub combo, which actually exists because of resale value mumbo jumbo and not because Americans LOVE shower tubs).)
- A mirror in the bathroom and a full-length mirror somewhere else.
- A washer. I, with a capital L, Love being able to wash and clean my clothes at will.
- Really fast wifi.
- Really cold AC in the bedroom.

So to get this, we arrive in Tirana and get to our accommodation and we can't find it. Turned out, and this is a real thing that happened, we needed to go into a mall and then into the Swatch store, and tell the Swatch employee we were here for the apartment. We then paid at the Swatch store and were led to a secret back elevator and given a key to our room. Us silly Americans really should know by now that if we can't find our accommodation, we obviously need to start questioning teenage mall employees. We'll know for next time.

## North Macedonia

Our next stop was just a bus ride away: Lake Ohrid. Due to some colonizers getting ahold of a pen and drawing some lines they shouldn't have, Albania's borders changed, and changed again: Ottomans, independence, inter-Balkan rifts, Western European meddling, a couple World Wars, communism (Stalin, the man really got around), and finally the Balkan Wars. Anyways, Lake Ohrid (like Shkodër Lake) used to be entirely contained within Albania's border, but both lakes now sit on the borderline. So depending on which shoreline you inhabit, you are either in Albania or North Macedonia.

Ryker and I opted for the North Macedonian side, specifically the town of Ohrid, as it's gorgeous, charming, quaint, and a UNESCO World Heritage Site (still not 100% sure who is deciding that label and, other than protection/tourism, what the point is). We also, in the most vain way possible, want to rack up our country count for this year to an impressive number. The goal is 30, and North Macedonia clocked in at number 19.

Ohrid was like taking a step back in time. Remember back in the day, before LEDs, when all the streetlamps had a yellow tinge to them? You could walk around at night and it was calm and quiet. I think of that one scene of the streetlamp in Harry Potter. Now streetlamps are bright white light, which I am told greatly decreased crime and was good for the environment, but was a total loss for the vibes. Ohrid was like stepping back into the yellow light. Young children and teenagers roamed the streets, wild and with the joy only a lack of supervision can provide. Parents and travelers alike filled the seats at restaurants lining the water, a cold beer in one hand, a cigarette in the other. Everyone was either coming from or going to the lake for a quick dip to cool down. The energy in this town was unlike anywhere else we had been. It felt homey, it felt foreign, it felt like a teenage coming-of-age movie, set in the time of sticky sweet watermelon running down your face, bare feet getting singed as they slap on cement, the feeling of exhilaration when you are sweaty and mid-air, right before the quiet cold water surrounds you.

Other than "lake time" and "wander around town time," I planned two other events for our week: a cultural walking tour and scuba diving. The cultural walking tour ended up being private, as Ryker and I were the only two who showed up. We got to meet copper artists and leather workers, see one of the seven original Gutenberg printing presses (which was super cool, and it still works), learn how to make paper the old-fashioned way, and meet the famous filigree families of Ohrid. Ohrid sells a lot of pearls, which is odd, given it's a freshwater lake, i.e. no pearl production. We learned an "Ohrid Pearl" is not like the pearls you or I think of: the ones born from oysters. These pearls are human-created via a secret family process, and only two families know the recipe. It's been passed down for generations (obviously only to male heirs, even though only women worked the shops, but I digress). They import shells and scrape out the insides, smush them into a ball and paint them with the magic secret recipe juice. To be fair, they do look like real pearls. I did not partake in obtaining any "pearl" knickknacks.

We had noticed it was crowded in Ohrid, but no more than many tourist hotspots. We learned that August 2nd is North Macedonia's 4th of July, and the entire country comes to Lake Ohrid for the holiday. So it was about to get a whole lot busier. On top of the crowds, heat records were being broken that week. The temperature was sitting at a pretty 95-100 the whole time; thankfully the lake stayed blissfully cold.

My last fun fact that we learned was that North Macedonia for the longest time was an exporter of white marble. The white marble exporters consistently had to throw out marble because there was this annoying pink hue ruining the perfect white consistency. Turned out that pink was much more valuable than the marble: it was ruby. Not the classic red ruby you'd think of, but a soft pink color. This pink hue (vs. red) is caused by more chrome in the makeup of the stone. Rubies and sapphires are the 2nd hardest stone, right after diamond. The pink "Macedonian Rubies" are unique to the region and are not found anywhere else in the world. Despite this, the government refuses to regulate the industry or have scientists come in and declare this stone its own thing. Because of that, the workers in the marble mines would just take this stuff home and sell it on the black market. In recent years, the tides seem to be turning and there is a push for regulation, but for now the lack of regulation means Ryker and I can pick up some rubies for cheap.

We ended up both getting rings at a family-run shop that also specializes in silver filigree. By family-run, I mean the whole family: we met the mom, dad, and daughter, all of whom make jewelry and work the store, and the daughter is even heading to a fancy jewelry and gemstone school in Italy! Silver filigree is an art where silver is melted and pulled through holes to make hair-thin strands that are then cut, bent, and welded into place to form beautiful intricate patterns. All by hand; it's impressive the tiny scale at which these folks are able to work. See pictures of our new pretty pretty rings below.

I booked us a scuba dive at a shop run by a father-son duo. Turns out, other than taking tourists down into the deep, their main business is training the world's militaries. Both father and son were buff bundles of muscle, both ex-military. Lake Ohrid is the fourth oldest lake in the world and the oldest in Europe, and has a maximum depth just shy of 1,000 ft. Other countries' versions of the Navy SEALs come at night and dive down, down, into the depths without light and practice military stuff.

This was Ryker's and my first time diving in freshwater. See, all of our other dives have been in the ocean, and the saltwater makes it much easier to control your buoyancy. When scuba diving, one of your main goals is to remain flat, or parallel to the ground, and kind of float around so you don't disturb anything. This was much harder than expected in Lake Ohrid. So after we went under with our dive master, I quite literally looked like a fish out of water. There are photos to prove it.

## Kosovo

Kosovo is a young country, having just gained independence from Serbia in 2008. They are still figuring some things out, like currency, and use the Euro as an interim. Kosovo is not yet acknowledged by the UN as a sovereign state, but the USA played a key role in backing Kosovo's independence. For this reason, the people of Kosovo are very pro-America and American flags are frequently seen flying.

Our journey to Kosovo was another bus, wait, 'nother bus situation. Janine graciously left us a mini portable fan that has quite literally been keeping us sane on these long travel days. It's a mixture of constant cigarette smoke plus failing AC that strikes to kill one's spirit. But this little fan is like a knight facing the dragon and keeping us sane.

## Montenegro

I will be the first to acknowledge that by this point in the trip I was tired of endless summer. I was ready for the cold, and I could picture in just a couple short weeks nipping about through the cool autumn weather in England. But not just yet; we were still in the midst of our Schengen Shuffle.

We arrived in Montenegro to a hotel room filled to the brim with cigarette smoke, and after a whole day on hot buses through winding roads, I was in no mood. Our hosts were accommodating, and we managed to get the room aired out in just under 4 hours, and we were determined to not let it affect our time in Ulcinj. We had two locations planned for Montenegro, and Ulcinj was chosen as it is a seaside town that sits atop steep cliffs that climb down to the ocean. Naturally we were at the top of the hill and took on the hilly descent once or twice a day the whole time we were there.

The entire coastline of Montenegro and the water was beautiful. White rocky beaches, crystal blue water, sweetly scented pine trees. We spent most days walking along the shore under the pines, finding different swimming holes and basking in the sun.

Our main adventure was going to the largest independent olive oil producer in the country, which naturally was the backyard just out of town. We got to meet the man himself and learn about the struggles of pruning the trees by hand and finding honest workers, and of course we purchased our own bottle of oil that was bottled on the spot. We got to see a wildfire take over a hill only about a mile away and were informed by the olive oil man that arson was common, as it was the cheapest way to clear land.

See, Montenegro is a nation that struggles with governmental corruption. It's a land of just over ½ a million people, governed by a billionaire's son who is set on making Montenegro the renewable energy producer of Europe and joining the EU by 2028. Wind and solar farms are abundant and Montenegro does see the lowest electricity cost in Europe, but that still didn't stop anyone from complaining about the cost, the land use, or the fact that all this energy might not benefit anyone locally. Only time will tell if the renewables bet will pay off, especially as we see Romania's Black Sea oil lines come online in 2028.

We woke at 5am and walked over a mile with our bags to the bus station, only to be told that the bus was not at 6am but had now changed times to 1:30pm. After some back-and-forth hassling, we found out we were lied to and the 6am bus was actually headed in the right direction, and we jumped on with only seconds to spare. I now have a deep-seated dislike for the bus network in Montenegro. Everyone who worked there seems to hate their jobs with a fury and be very willing to take it out on anyone dumb enough to try to actually use the buses to get from point A to point B. If they had it their way, we really should just come, pay, leave, and then go and pay again for a rental car. Alas, we are on a budget, so we slogged through the rude remarks and the extra fees: to have a bag, to have a paper ticket, to be in the bus station prior to boarding the bus, to sit down, to breathe air, to drink our own water, and to get off at the correct stop. With all these extra fees, we made it to our next destination only 5 hours late, which is practically on time for Montenegrins.

We arrived at our second city in Montenegro, Herceg Novi, to by far one of the worst accommodations of our trip. Paper-thin mattresses, naturally no blankets, barely-held-together threads that I guess counted as towels. No worries, though: the kitchen and bathroom were filthy, the "shower" was a knee-high spout in the wall, naturally no hot water, but the AC did work incredibly well (Western Europe should take notes: no potable H2O, but they have AC). Long story short, we survived and there were no bedbugs.

Our main adventures in Herceg Novi consisted of swimming in the beautiful Adriatic Sea and wandering around town. We had much joyous fun observing our fellow tourists in various states of dress striding to and from the beach while enjoying some sorbet. We risked it all and ate some fresh mussels and did not get sick (a total win).

Have I mentioned our bad luck with grocery stores? I must have, at some point, royally pissed off the grocery store mafia. In Georgia I was yelled at for not translating what I was saying into Russian (they speak Georgian, color me confused). I have been told many times that I am not allowed to buy fruit, that it is "not for me." Upon trying to buy lotion, I was told that "it was impossible" and they do not sell that. I had handed them a bottle of lotion, from their shelves, to buy, and they placed it behind the counter and said, while smiling, that they sell no lotion. Herceg Novi might take the cake. I attempted to buy laundry detergent, a small travel pack, maybe 12 pods overall, listed for €4.30. Our scanner lady looked at the product and said it was impossible; we could not buy it. I went and took a photo of the price tag and showed it to her, for her to key in the price. Nope, still impossible. Silly me, I should know, laundry detergent isn't for Americans, duh. We went back and forth; naturally there is now a massive line behind me, and I am apologizing to everyone in line. The man next to me was not surprised and said this was a usual occurrence. The lady finally compromised: she would sell me the detergent for €39. I took my laundry business elsewhere.

Ryker's birthday fell while we were here, and the funny thing about traveling the world for a whole year is that every day is special and novel. So making a birthday extra special is quite a challenge. Gifts were out, as we are on a specifically allocated budget and, more importantly, we only have so much space in our bags. I settled on a boat tour around the Bay of Kotor.

A couple exciting things happened. One, we got to watch a helicopter go back and forth (and back and forth) putting out a wildfire with a massive amount of water, and we got to watch the pilot entirely miss the fire one time, like they were off by an entire mountain. Incredibly embarrassing if you ask me, but they are saving lives and all that, so we shall give them the benefit of the doubt. Our tour gave us mystery shots at 9:43 in the morning, and for some godforsaken reason we took them (on empty stomachs), and let me tell you, that is not the move. Absolutely abysmal use of calories. We got maybe the best photo ever taken of us, airdropped to us by a stranger; it could be a movie poster (see below). Finally we got to wander the town of Kotor, and while it has tiny winding streets and a walled town, it's also just a lot of tourists on tour. Kotor is a stop for many cruise ships, so souvenir shops galore. Kotor, if you research going to Montenegro, is one of those "Must See" "hidden gem" places. Let me set the record straight and say you do not have to go. It is a very old town, it's crowded, it's beautiful and right on the water, but it's not special. Herceg Novi was much more delightful.

## Bosnia: Mostar

Bosnia was another last-minute decision. We had originally planned on staying in Montenegro for longer, but honestly, we were ready to get the hell outta Dodge. Also, juxtaposed with its reputation as budget backpacking central, I found the Balkans to be quite expensive, and really not worth the money. It's the cost of Western Europe but with half the quality.

Anyways, Bosnia: we were excited and initially booked two towns to split our week, but ended up loving Mostar so much that we canceled our second town and remained in Mostar the whole time.

Bosnia was involved in the bloody Yugoslav Wars, 1991-2001, the classic story of once you remove the communist (*cough* authoritarian) leader, the whole fragile system falls apart. There was this big dog, Tito, who held Serbia, Croatia, Bosnia, Slovenia, Macedonia, and Montenegro together, but when he died, there was a power vacuum and all those nations started to declare independence. The problem was people had moved, so now you had different loyalties, nationalities, ethnicities, and religions vying for the same area. (This is really simplified; the whole thing is much more complicated, so go pick up a book if you want to learn more.) The game of "who gets what" was a bloody one, and Bosnia was hit quite hard: ethnic cleansings and mass executions. Mostar sustained the most damage of all the cities in Bosnia.

As Ryker and I walked around, you could actually see bullet holes in many of the buildings. Venturing further, there were blocks of nice houses and then one that was completely empty, firebombed out and left to rot.

The Stari Most is the most famous bridge; if you look up Mostar, it's the first image you will see. It was rebuilt after its destruction and is now used by Red Bull for their World Cliff Diving championships. It's an 80ft jump, so the impact is similar to slamming into concrete. After a couple overzealous tourists hurt themselves, some rules on who can make the jump were instituted. Now one must go through a course with the city's diving club, and they decide who can make the leap. In that vein, the diving club built a couple of shorter starter platforms for folks to practice, and lucky for Ryker and me, those jumps are free for all.

### The Luck of the Ryker

Ryker is intrinsically athletic and has taken a liking to jumping off things into bodies of water. Mostar was his playground: not only could he jump off a cliff, but there was a crowd of people to watch and cheer him on for every jump. So he got to practice his jumping, hair tossing, and crowd waving all in one. To make the jump, one must enter fairly cold water, swim across a fast-flowing river, and climb a rock ledge to reach a platform that had two heights for jumping. Many hostel hoppers can be seen lounging on the rocks below, watching jumpers and waiting for their go.

Ryker did stun the crowd, but not in the way he would have liked. After completing his jump, he popped up and was chatting with me on the rock when Idiot Number One jumped without looking, landing inches from Ryker's head. The crowd went silent. Hands over mouths at what had almost occurred. Luckily Ryker was fine, but Idiot One (the jumper) and Idiot Two (the friend who told him the coast was clear) were both given a talking-to.
