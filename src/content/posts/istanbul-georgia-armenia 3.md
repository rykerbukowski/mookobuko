It's official — our Euro Summer has started. After chasing summer throughout Asia, we high-tailed it to Europe, just narrowly escaping before the big bad monsoon season hit. I have never been more thankful to be out of the wet-towel humidity blanket we endured. We started out in Istanbul for 48 hours before heading to Georgia. We had a plan that we needed to make a plan. We were planning to do some planning — but after Istanbul.

Europe, America's Playground, has many, many benefits and three big cons: cost, competition, and air conditioning. The first two can be easily solved for most vacations with time and by choosing one's location carefully. While Ryker and I can be choosy about our locations, time in this sense is not something we have. We have settled into an operating rhythm of booking our accommodations, transport, and activities about 48 hours before we need them. This is not possible in Europe — the closer to the date of arrival, the higher the costs, and the more tourists, and therefore higher prices (that Intro to Econ course coming in handy). It turns out when constantly traveling the world, there are no breaks and no time to plan what to do next. That time needs to be carefully carved out into our daily schedule. We knew we needed to dedicate time in Georgia to planning ahead.

Georgia has been on my list for a while now, after my best friend Sofia went in 2018. It also doubles as one of the least expensive, least touristed places in Europe — by this I mean all the tourists are Russian. Ok yes, Georgia is technically on the continent of Asia, and is technically not Europe, or the Middle East, or Asia. It is its own distinct region: the Caucasus. But you get my grouping. It was the perfect place to explore and get some chill time in to plan.

Our second hurdle: air conditioning. AC has become very near and dear to my little American heart. In Asia it was needed to survive, and everyone has it. The $20-a-night hotel? They have AC. It might be tiny and 20 years old, but it cools down the room in 20 minutes flat. Europe, for some godforsaken reason, has a massive stick up its ass about how they are "better" than us Americans and how they "don't need AC" because this is the natural way their ancestors lived. However, global warming is kicking European ass, and fun fact: more Europeans die each year from heat stroke (175,000 annually) than Americans die from gun violence. That's right — the Americans and our right to bear arms may cause death in South Chicago, but European snobby stubbornness and superiority complex causes mass death that's solvable with a technology that's existed for decades. It doesn't help that they shut down all their nuclear energy plants and divorced themselves from their ability to generate their own energy, especially shooting themselves in the foot — which they managed to do without guns. Now the great and powerful empires of Europe keep whining about how mean Russia is and how they're being bullied by Russian oil oligarchs. I don't feel bad for them at all. Heat stroke is way more preventable than gun death. I mean, America's right to bear arms is in our Constitution (hard to revoke) and the European right to not adopt new technologies is not enshrined in any governmental document — it's just a social norm, and a stupid one.

BUT but but but but — this stupid European norm now affects me and my delicate little princess self that needs a cool 64 to 67°F room to promote optimal sleep. My sleep is very important to me. We found out in our first couple of days in Georgia (which is getting lumped into the EU for the AC thing) that people LOVE to lie about having AC. We filter our accommodations and all the Airbnb overlords say they have AC and don't — or worse, they do have it, but it's in the living room or bathroom or the freaking porch, not the bedroom. Why, and I mean WHY, would I care about AC in the living room and not the bedroom? What backwards thinking. But that is the great evolved European mind for you.

Anyways, I am very grateful for my life. It's a privilege to travel around the world with the love of my life and I have no real problems. Heavy is the head that wears the crown, all that. I will stop complaining and limit my jabs and tell you, dear reader, about what we got up to this month.

## Istanbul

We went to Istanbul for one reason and one reason only. Ryker found business class tickets from South Korea to Turkey for a reasonable point redemption. We didn't care where in Europe we ended up post-Korea, and Istanbul was as good a spot as any. I'll fly wherever if it's in business class. You could probably ship me off to Iran if you tempted me with first class.

Sadly — it's true — business class was everything I thought it would be and more. We were treated like humans. Given goodie bags. Treated to multiple multi-course meals. Unlimited access to alcoholic and non-alcoholic drinks. The flight attendants acted like they were happy we were there and even smiled at us and cracked a few jokes, instead of treating us like annoying slugs taking up space and getting in their way, as is the normal treatment in economy. We actually arrived rested and ready to start the day after 17 hours of flying, instead of sad little shrimp timidly recovering from the horrors of being sardined in. I never want to go back to economy, but I will — I'm not made of money. This experience did give me something to strive for though. A reason to make those cold calls when I get back to work.

## Georgia Part 1: Wine & More Wine

We arrived in Georgia's capital Tbilisi in perfect weather: soft sunshine. Say what you will, but first impressions of a place stick, and this one stuck. We left the airport with our noses pressed to the glass of our cab, watching hills roll by and vast stretches of green fade into mountains. The capital itself seems to drip out of the mountainous cliffs that support the southern side. A river splits the city in two — all winding streets and glass bridges.

### The Wine Tour

Georgia is known to many as the birthplace of wine, having the oldest evidence of winemaking in the world, dating back 8,000 years. Naturally, wine is a big part of Georgian culture, and Kakheti — one of the major wine regions — bubbled up to the top of my list for places to visit.

Wine is also one of the most gatekept industries ever. You've got to know somebody to tour a vineyard, or dedicate lots of time to learning about wine and the winemakers of a region, pick from thousands where you want to go, and then somehow contact them to schedule a visit. We chose the easier route: have enough money to pay the person who knows someone, and then you too can be in the know.

We toured with "Eat This!" — a tour company I found via a blog called Wander-Lush. (Side note: this blog was my holy grail for planning Georgia and Armenia.) Our group consisted of us, a retired Australian couple who themselves owned a vineyard, and our tour guide, translator, and driver. We quickly got on very well with our partners in drinking, and like most Australians we've met, they were incredibly well traveled, so we had lots to talk about. We drove through a picturesque valley — cows roaming, vast green fields, and snow-topped Caucasus Mountains framing the distance.

We arrived at our first of three wineries and started our journey of learning about wine. Most of my actual learning came from our fellow tourists. Over the next two days they helped me understand tannins (the stuff that gets into wine when it's made with grape skins on) and why they are more oriented toward an older palate. The difference between white wine and red wine is that red is usually made with the skins on, and the color difference comes from the skins — not from green versus purple grapes. But not in Georgia. In Georgia there is white, amber, and black wine. White is the usual. Amber is made from green grapes but with the skin left on. Black is red. Georgians also uniquely age wine in giant underground clay vessels called qvevri, and they leave the skins on during this process.

Anyways, we also learned there are no alcoholics in Georgia, because everyone is — you know — not crossing that line, but getting real close. Safe to say the wines we tried were poured with a heavy hand. By the time we made it to the third winery we had already been drinking for hours, and not just wine. After the grape skins are used to make the wine, they are separated out and further distilled to make chacha, a hard liqueur closer to 40 to 50% ABV. Our final stop included a Supra — a Georgian feast in a family home — where many toasts are made by the toastmaster, and it's customary to finish your glass after each toast. None of that cheers-and-one-sip business. I'm talking cheers and chug. Naturally Ryker and I enjoyed much merriment, much to the chagrin of our future selves.

Everything we had hit. My favorite was shkmeruli — chicken in a very garlicky cream sauce that paired perfectly with Georgian bread.

## Armenia

Being in the area and all, we decided to make a quick five-day jaunt down to Armenia. Given the proximity of the countries, we decided to try something new: a land crossing. We hired a driver for the four-hour drive and programmed in about four stops at famous bluffs and monasteries.

Our first stop was the border. Which, unlike the USA/Canada crossing, is actually two different borders. We first exited Georgia — getting out of the car, standing in line, answering questions — then got back in the car and drove 500 feet. Got back out of the car and entered Armenia, where we were asked about our relationship to Azerbaijan. We have never been, and now cannot go until we get new passports. Neighbors Armenia and Azerbaijan hate each other and are at war over disputed territory. The tea is that both are former Soviet states, and rumor has it the beef was instigated by the Soviets — with Russia now fanning the flames.

## Georgia Part 2: Cheating on Ocha with a New (to Us) RAV4

Fun fact: if you go to Georgia and ask the internet whether you should rent a car, you will get a resounding answer of "hell no." Apparently the roads are bad, the drivers are crazy, and Google Maps is unreliable. I file this advice into the general complaining that everyone has about literally every place. Did you know that every city has the "worst drivers," the "worst traffic," and also the "best food" and is the most "gastronomical city in X country"? Brother, if I have to hear that one more time. Can we all just agree that humans in general are bad drivers — I can even hold your hand when I tell you AI will be and already is better — and that all cities generally have pretty good food? So with all our travel wisdom, we still rented a car. And... it was totally normal. A couple of cows here and there, Google Maps worked mostly, and drivers behaved better than average in our opinion. We would HIGHLY recommend renting a car in Georgia. I have a conspiracy theory that all the fear-mongering online about Georgian driving is actually peddled by the taxi drivers of Georgia.

We landed back in Tbilisi ready for our road trip. We had booked a car and expected to pick it up at the airport. Sadly, the man running the car rental forgot about us, so we sat like sad, forgotten children in the terminal while he sent a taxi to bring us to the car.

We rented a 2016 RAV4 to give us a taste of home — we had been missing our 2024 RAV4 that is currently being babysat by my father. Let me tell you, we had not touched a steering wheel in six months and it felt oh-so-good to be mobile. The world was at our fingertips. We could go anywhere. So naturally we went to the birthplace of Stalin: Gori. Fun fact: Stalin — the Joseph Stalin, mass murderer, socialist, communist, and leader of the Soviet Union — was Georgian. Legend has it that he kept his Georgian accent when speaking Russian because he felt it made him more intimidating. He also read over 20,000 books in his lifetime. We know he actually read them because he annotated each one, and those are only the ones we know about. Assuming he started reading at age six, that's 294 books a year — five a week. Puts me to shame with my measly one book a week.
