Tokyo hit differently than anywhere we'd been.

The city has a texture to it that doesn't translate — the way every block contains its own world, the obsessive precision of every storefront, the vending machine every thirty meters. You don't experience Tokyo so much as get absorbed by it.

We arrived with a loose agenda: eat well, find the teamLab, pick up a few things we'd been putting off since Bangkok. The eating happened immediately. The first night we landed in a yakiniku spot with a ventilation hood for every table and a menu we ordered by pointing.

Onomichi is the kind of place that makes you feel like you've wandered into a film.

It sits at the edge of the Seto Inland Sea, stacked up a hillside, all narrow lanes and Buddhist temples and cats sleeping on stone walls. The city has been slowly decaying in the most beautiful possible way for decades — old buildings converted into cafes, fishing boats rusting in the harbor, a ropeway that climbs to a viewpoint where the whole thing unfolds below you like a painting.

We had one day here before the ride. We used it well.

The Shimanami Kaido is a 70-kilometer cycling route connecting Onshu on the Japanese mainland to Imabari on Shikoku, island-hopping across six bridges over the Seto Inland Sea. It's been called the most beautiful cycling route in the world.

That's not wrong.

We accidentally rode through a festival on a tiny island and stayed for two songs.

We took a ferry across to Matsuyama — the largest city on Shikoku — for a night.

The plan was simple: get to the Dogo Onsen. It's the oldest hot spring in Japan, possibly the oldest in all of recorded Japanese history. There's a theory that it's the bathhouse that inspired Spirited Away. There's no way to verify this, but standing in a yukata in a wood-and-tile room that's been steaming since 700 AD, it doesn't feel wrong.

Korea was Meg's idea.

The Four Rivers Cycling Route — 국토종주 자전거길 — runs from Seoul to Busan, following four rivers through the heart of the Korean peninsula. 633 kilometers. Stamped checkpoints every fifty kilometers. An official completion certificate waiting at the end in Busan.

It is also absolutely not a casual bike ride.

We rented bikes from a shop in Seoul, loaded them with panniers, and rolled out of Gwangnaru Bicycle Park on a Tuesday morning feeling excellent about the whole thing.

Asia had been six months. Japan, Korea, Thailand, Vietnam, the Philippines, Bali, Indonesia. The cycling. The temples. The food. The hospital visits and the onsen and the 633 kilometers.

There's a specific feeling on the other side of something that took everything you had — not relief exactly, not sadness, something more like quiet. Like the world got a little larger and you got a little smaller and both of those things feel fine.

We flew west. There was more.
